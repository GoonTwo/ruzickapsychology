“Doing the work” appears everywhere in conversations about mental health. It can sound important while remaining frustratingly vague. Does it mean journaling, practicing a coping skill, talking about childhood, or simply attending therapy every week?

In therapy, the work is not a single exercise. It is the collaborative process of noticing patterns, understanding what those patterns have helped you manage, and trying new ways of responding with care.

## In individual therapy: moving from autopilot to awareness

Individual therapy can create room to slow down and understand experiences that are difficult to make sense of alone. The work may involve naming emotions, noticing how the body responds to stress, examining recurring relationship patterns, or practicing skills for difficult moments.

Insight and practical tools can support one another. Understanding why a response developed does not automatically change it, and using a skill does not require ignoring the history behind the response.

## In perinatal therapy: making room for a major transition

Pregnancy, birth, loss, and early parenthood can involve significant physical, relational, and identity changes. Perinatal work is not about becoming a “perfect” parent.

Sessions may include exploring anxiety or intrusive thoughts, grieving an experience that differed from expectations, examining inherited ideas about parenting, and making room for a changing sense of self. A clinician can also help distinguish common adjustment concerns from symptoms that may need additional support.

## In couples therapy: understanding the cycle

Couples often arrive focused on the latest argument. The work can include identifying the larger cycle around that argument: how each person reacts, what each response communicates, and how both partners become pulled into a pattern neither one wants.

In Imago-informed couples therapy, structured dialogue can help partners slow the exchange down, reflect what they hear, and respond with greater clarity. The process does not ask partners to agree about everything. It creates a more deliberate way to understand the experience on both sides.

## The work is collaborative

Therapy does not require a perfect agenda or the right words. It begins with showing up as you are and becoming curious about what keeps you feeling stuck, anxious, depleted, or disconnected.
